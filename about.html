Research Foundation and Supporting Evidence

Course Concepts

For our project, we drew on several course modules: memory and learning, neuroplasticity, nature vs. nurture, executive functions, and the social brain. The memory and learning module, along with the Memory Hackers documentary, introduced the different types of memory and the brain regions responsible for forming, storing, and retrieving them. For example, the case of patient H.M. showed that damage to the hippocampus can impair the ability to form new long-term memories while leaving other systems, such as working memory and procedural memory, largely intact. This suggests that memory is not a single construct but a set of separate systems, which aging may affect in different ways. The neuroplasticity and nature vs. nurture modules showed that the brain keeps changing throughout life and that both genetic and environmental factors shape how it develops and functions. We also learned that exercise and sleep can support neuroplasticity, while chronic stress can impair it. Together, these modules suggest that although some memory decline is a normal part of aging, lifestyle choices can play an important role in maintaining brain health and memory over time.

The executive functions module is also relevant. It covered working memory and attention, and how sleep, stress, and physical health can affect them. The social brain module showed that social connection and interaction are essential for healthy brain function. This links directly to our project, since social isolation has been identified as a modifiable risk factor for dementia (Livingston et al., 2024). Overall, these course concepts give us a foundation for understanding how different brain regions support memory and how outside factors influence it. We can apply this knowledge in our project to help middle-aged adults build healthier habits early, with the goal of reducing cognitive decline as they age.

Additional Resources

Exercise, BDNF, and Neuroplasticity in Older Adults

Erickson et al. (2011) showed that lifestyle habits can physically change the aging brain. In a randomized controlled trial with 120 inactive adults aged 55 to 80, one year of moderate aerobic exercise (walking) increased the size of the hippocampus, a key memory region, by about 2%. That reversed roughly 1 to 2 years of age-related volume loss. The growth was linked to higher levels of BDNF, a protein that supports the growth of new neurons. The study's age range partly overlaps with our midlife audience, so it strengthens our proposal by showing that the brain stays plastic later in life. It gives a scientific basis for the exercise habit in our tracker.

Dementia Risk Factors

Livingston et al. (2024) reviewed evidence across the lifespan and identified 14 potentially modifiable risk factors for dementia, including hearing loss, hypertension, physical inactivity, depression, and social isolation. They estimated that about 45% of dementia cases could be prevented or delayed if all 14 factors were eliminated. This estimate assumes that the factors are causal and can be fully removed. Hearing loss and high LDL cholesterol, both midlife factors, had the largest impact of any single factor. These findings support our focus on midlife, when many of the most important risk factors appear. That makes it a key window for building habits that protect memory later in life.

Normal vs. Abnormal Aging

Nyberg et al. (2019) contrast normal memory aging with early Alzheimer's disease. Healthy older adults take longer to learn new information, but once it is well consolidated, they forget it no faster than younger adults. In contrast, patients with early Alzheimer's lost about 75% of newly learned material after just a 2-minute delay. Biologically, age-related tissue loss mainly affects white matter rather than grey matter. This means the connections between neurons slow down, rather than the neurons themselves being destroyed. Neuron loss in the hippocampus is also selective and spares its core processing regions. PET data suggest the hippocampus activates similarly in younger and older adults, while older adults show different frontal activation. This may reflect different retrieval strategies, such as more effortful searching or using both sides of the prefrontal cortex. Early Alzheimer's, by contrast, involves heavy neuron loss in the entorhinal cortex, the gateway that sends new information into the hippocampus. This distinction matters for our project: we can reassure users that slower learning is a normal part of aging, while explaining why rapid forgetting is a warning sign worth raising with a doctor.

Significance and Impact

Significance of the Project

Our project is unique because it targets adults in midlife, before memory problems appear, rather than older adults or caregivers, who are the usual focus of dementia resources. It fills two key gaps. First, many people confuse normal age-related forgetfulness with dementia, which can cause unnecessary worry. By explaining that different memory systems age differently, we help our audience tell the difference. Second, people often know that exercise, sleep, and blood pressure matter for their general health, but not that these same factors shape their future brain health. Our website and habit tracker link each habit to the neuroscience behind it, such as neuroplasticity and exercise-driven hippocampal growth, turning research into simple daily actions. Together, these show that it is never too early, or too late, to protect your memory.

Predicted Impact

We predict that our project will clear up common misunderstandings and change how middle-aged adults understand and act on their cognitive health. For understanding, we expect our audience to better distinguish normal age-related changes, such as mild forgetfulness and working memory decline, from the signs of dementia. We also expect them to learn which habits can help lower dementia risk. Together, these should reduce unnecessary worry. For behaviour, we think that linking each habit to its neuroscience mechanism (for example, how an active lifestyle can increase the size of the hippocampus) will help our audience see the direct connection between daily choices and brain health. This should motivate them to adopt evidence-based habits that protect their memory as they age.
